## 2. Comparison of Existing Techniques

### 2.1 Facial Recognition and Face Anti-Spoofing
Deep learning is widely used in facial recognition and face anti-spoofing. The selected studies include CNN-based methods, Siamese networks, gradient-based approaches and Vision Transformers. Pei et al. (2023) use a person-specific Siamese network with a genuine reference image, while Wang et al. (2023) use a learnable gradient operator. Shu et al. (2023) use multi-scale colour inversion and Solomon and Cios (2023) combine image-quality features with deep learning. These studies show that different visual and identity-related cues can improve spoof detection, but performance remains affected by input quality, attack characteristics and testing conditions.

### 2.2 Cross-Domain and Unseen Attack Detection
Cai et al. (2022), Wang et al. (2022), Zhang and Nie (2023) and Huang et al. (2023) address domain variation using meta pattern learning, fine-grained patch recognition, feature alignment and test-time adaptation. These approaches improve generalisation, but large differences between training and target conditions can still reduce performance. Test-time adaptation can also introduce additional computation and latency.

### 2.3 Deepfakes and Adversarial Attacks
Zafar et al. (2025) combine spatial and temporal deep learning features for deepfake detection, while Belguesmia et al. (2025) use multiple facial views and pose information. Hwang et al. (2023) show that GAN-generated adversarial patches can affect black-box face recognition systems. Liu et al. (2025) review the shift from GAN-based to diffusion-based deepfakes. Together, these studies show that attack diversity and changing generation methods remain important robustness considerations.

### 2.4 Privacy and Protection of Facial Biometric Data
Shahreza and Marcel (2023) demonstrate the risk of template inversion, while Osorio-Roig et al. (2022) show that privacy-enhanced facial representations may still expose sensitive attributes. Sardar et al. (2023), Wang et al. (2022), Hu et al. (2024) and Huang et al. (2024) use different protection mechanisms at the template, frequency, encrypted-processing or feature-learning stages. The studies show that privacy protection can reduce exposure, but it can also introduce processing cost or affect recognition performance.

### 2.5 Federated, Blockchain and Secure-Computing Approaches
Woubie et al. (2024) use federated learning to keep raw data on clients. Ghani et al. (2024) combine GAN-based obfuscation, clustering and blockchain, while Chen et al. (2023) use additive secret sharing and secure multi-party computation. These approaches reduce direct exposure of facial information, but communication, storage, and processing overhead remain important for constrained deployment.

### 2.6 Multimodal and Practical Systems
Yu et al. (2024) combine RGB, infrared and depth information using multimodal deep learning, while Gimba et al. (2025) combine face and fingerprint recognition. Ahmad et al. (2024) and Zainuddin et al. (2024) demonstrate practical facial recognition for attendance and smaller computing platforms. These studies support the feasibility of deployment, but added sensors, data or hardware increase complexity, and recognition accuracy alone does not provide a complete security evaluation.

## 4. Research Gap Analysis

Based on the reviewed studies, four connected gaps are identified.

### 4.1 Cross-domain presentation attack detection remains challenging
Meta-learning, patch-level recognition, style assembly, feature alignment and test-time adaptation improve generalisation, but substantial differences between training and target conditions can still affect performance. This supports the proposed focus on cross-domain liveness detection.

### 4.2 Presentation attack detection and biometric privacy are commonly studied separately
The PAD literature mainly focuses on detecting whether a presentation is genuine or an attack, while privacy research focuses on protecting facial images, features or templates. The reviewed studies provide useful individual solutions, but fewer address both concerns together in one practical authentication framework.

### 4.3 Privacy-preserving approaches can introduce computational or communication requirements
Encryption, secure multi-party computation, blockchain and federated learning offer different forms of protection, but their overhead is an important consideration for edge devices. This creates a practical trade-off between security, privacy and execution efficiency.

### 4.4 Practical recognition systems do not always evaluate complete biometric security
Application-oriented systems can report high recognition accuracy without fully evaluating cross-domain spoofing and privacy together. This suggests a gap between good recognition performance and the requirements of a secure, private and lightweight biometric authentication system.

### 4.5 Main research gap
The main gap is the limited integration of cross-domain presentation attack detection, biometric template privacy and computational efficiency within a single practical framework. The proposed research addresses this gap by investigating existing PAD and privacy mechanisms, designing a lightweight combined framework, and evaluating both PAD error and execution latency. This is an integration and evaluation gap rather than a claim that the individual components are absent from previous research.

## 6. Relevant Datasets and Data Sources

The selected literature uses a range of benchmark, public and application-oriented data sources. Common examples identified in the review include **CASIA-FASD, SiW, Replay-Attack, LFW, MOBIO, FFIW-10K, FaceForensics++, OpenForensics, CVL, IITK, CASIA-Face-v5, FERET, CelebA, HDU, CFP-FP, AgeDB-30, IJB-C, CPLFW, CALFW, QMUL-SurvFace**, and other public or custom datasets. Some studies also use **private/custom facial datasets, IoT-oriented data, or benchmark collections selected for specific application settings**.

For the proposed research, the literature supports selecting datasets that allow controlled comparison of source and target domains and provide appropriate genuine and attack samples for PAD evaluation. The exact final dataset choice is determined by the methodology and scope stated in Chapter 1 rather than by treating every reviewed dataset as a requirement.

## 7. Evaluation Metrics Identified in Previous Research

| Metric | Use in the reviewed literature | Relevance to proposed research |
|---|---|---|
| ACER | Used in face anti-spoofing studies to summarise attack and bona fide classification errors. | Supports evaluation of overall PAD error. |
| APCER | Measures error associated with presentation attacks. | Relevant for reporting attack-detection performance in PAD. |
| BPCER | Measures error associated with bona fide presentations. | Relevant for identifying false rejection of genuine users. |
| HTER | Used by several PAD studies as an error measure. | Useful as a supporting PAD metric when comparing related work. |
| EER | Used in biometric and PAD studies to compare false acceptance and false rejection behaviour. | Useful for biometric performance comparison where applicable. |
| Accuracy / Precision / Recall / F1-score | Commonly used for recognition, deepfake, masked-face and general classification studies. | Provides additional comparison context, but should not replace PAD-specific measures. |
| AUC / ROC-AUC | Used to measure discrimination performance in deepfake and face-recognition studies. | Useful where the selected model or experiment produces ROC-based evaluation. |
| Recognition accuracy / TAR@FAR | Used in facial recognition and privacy-preserving recognition studies. | Useful for checking whether privacy protection affects recognition utility. |
| Execution latency / inference time | Considered important in lightweight and practical deployment studies. | Directly relevant to evaluating computational efficiency on workstation and edge environments. |
